# Welfare Implications of Information Asymmetry (信息不对称的福利影响)

Type: rationale | Subject: unknown

Mathematical Definition:
For adverse selection, the expected cost of insurance is calculated as the average risk in the pooled market. In Chapter 22, the equilibrium premium \( p^* \) satisfies:
\[
p^* = \gamma \cdot MC^{\delta} + (1-\gamma) \cdot MC^{\theta}
\]
where \(\gamma\) is the proportion of low-risk (\(\delta\)), \(MC^{\delta}\) is marginal cost of insuring \(\delta\), and \(MC^{\theta}\) for high-risk (\(\theta\)).
Information asymmetry leads to market failures because it distorts decisions and reduces social welfare:
1. Inefficiency from Adverse Selection:
In the grade insurance example, low-risk students (e.g., B or C grades) drop out as premiums rise to cover high-risk students (F or D), reducing overall welfare.
Formal result: The market may collapse entirely if adverse selection is severe (e.g., no equilibrium exists for A-insurance).
2. Moral Hazard Costs:
Behavioral changes (e.g., reduced effort after obtaining insurance) increase insurers' costs, raising premiums further and reducing participation.
insurers use group averages (e.g., gender, race) to set prices due to lack of individual information. This can be rational (e.g., women live longer) but perpetuates inequalities.
Mathematical Example:
In the self-selecting equilibrium for insurance (Section 22B.2), low-risk types accept less-than-full coverage to signal their type. For \(\delta = 0.25\), optimal contract satisfies:
\[
U^{\delta}(b, p) = \alpha \delta \ln(x_1 + b - p) + \alpha (1-\delta) \ln(x_2 - p)
\]
and the contract \((b, p)\) solves this maximization problem under constraints.
Solutions and implications include:
Individuals reveal private information through costly signals (e.g., education as a signal of productivity).
Example: Low-risk drivers might purchase safety features to signal their type.
Insurers invest in information (e.g., credit checks, medical exams) to distinguish types.
Welfare impact: Screening costs may offset gains, but can restore efficiency if costs are low.
3. Market Institutions:
Reputational Markets: Brands and warranties reduce information asymmetry (e.g., certified used cars).
Third-Party Verification: Institutions like Consumer Reports provide independent quality assessments.
4. Government Intervention:
Mandatory Coverage: Requires all individuals to buy insurance (e.g., health insurance) to avoid adverse selection.
Anti-Discrimination Laws: Prohibit pricing based on protected attributes (e.g., race, gender), but may reduce efficiency if statistical discrimination is rational.
Welfare Result:
Information asymmetry reduces total welfare due to reduced trade and moral hazard (e.g., the "net loss" in Figure 22-1(c)).
Solutions like signaling/screening can improve welfare but often involve trade-offs (costs of signaling, reduced efficiency if policies misallocate resources).
