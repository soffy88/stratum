# Divisors, Line Bundles, and Picard Groups

This concept collects the main language Hartshorne uses to study codimension-one geometry on varieties and schemes: divisors, invertible sheaves (line bundles), and their classification via the Picard group. It is one of the central bridges between local algebra, global geometry, and cohomology in [[concepts/schemes]] and [[concepts/cohomology-in-algebraic-geometry]].

## Core idea

A divisor records where a rational function has zeros and poles, while a line bundle records the same kind of data in sheaf-theoretic form. The Picard group measures line bundles up to isomorphism, and therefore organizes the geometry of divisors modulo principal ambiguity.

In Hartshorne’s development, these ideas appear first for curves and then for higher-dimensional varieties and schemes, where the distinction between Weil and Cartier divisors becomes important.

## Divisors

### Weil divisors
On an integral scheme that is regular in codimension one, a **Weil divisor** is a formal integer combination of codimension-one irreducible subvarieties.

- Prime divisors are codimension-one integral closed subschemes.
- A Weil divisor is a finite sum
  \[
  D = \sum n_i Z_i.
  \]
- Effective divisors are those with all \(n_i \ge 0\).

A nonzero rational function \(f\) defines a **principal divisor** \((f)\), recording the orders of vanishing and poles along prime divisors. Principal divisors form a subgroup of all divisors.

### Linear equivalence
Two divisors are **linearly equivalent** if their difference is principal. This is the divisor-theoretic relation that identifies divisors coming from the same rational function class.

This equivalence is fundamental on curves and surfaces, where it leads to divisor class groups and ultimately to the geometry of linear systems.

## Line bundles / invertible sheaves

A **line bundle** is an invertible sheaf of modules, i.e. a locally free sheaf of rank one.

Hartshorne repeatedly uses the equivalence between:

- geometric data of divisors,
- algebraic data of invertible sheaves,
- and global sections of those sheaves.

A Cartier divisor determines an invertible sheaf, and in good situations the converse also holds. Thus line bundles are the sheaf-theoretic form of divisorial geometry.

### From divisors to sheaves
A Cartier divisor can be described locally by rational functions; its associated invertible sheaf is built from those local data.

This viewpoint is especially useful because:

- tensor products of line bundles correspond to addition of divisors,
- dual line bundles correspond to negation,
- effective divisors correspond to nonzero global sections.

## Picard group

The **Picard group** \(\mathrm{Pic}(X)\) is the group of isomorphism classes of invertible sheaves on \(X\), with tensor product as the group law.

It is the natural home for line bundle classification.

### Why it matters
The Picard group packages many geometric questions:

- Which divisors are linearly equivalent?
- Which line bundles have nonzero sections?
- When does a line bundle define a morphism to projective space?
- Which line bundles are ample or very ample?

The Picard group is especially important in the study of:

- curves, where degree controls much of the geometry,
- surfaces, where intersection theory interacts with divisor classes,
- and projective varieties, where ample line bundles govern embeddings.

## Weil vs. Cartier divisors

Hartshorne distinguishes two divisor theories:

- **Weil divisors**: defined by codimension-one subvarieties;
- **Cartier divisors**: defined locally by one rational function.

On a locally factorial or nonsingular scheme, these notions coincide. In that case, divisor theory and line bundle theory agree very cleanly.

When singularities are present, they can differ, so the Cartier viewpoint via line bundles becomes the more flexible language.

## Relation to divisor class groups

The **divisor class group** is divisors modulo principal divisors. It measures the failure of every divisor to be principal.

For normal schemes, Hartshorne shows a close relationship between:

- the divisor class group,
- the group of Cartier divisors modulo principal divisors,
- and the Picard group.

In favorable cases, the Picard group can be identified with the divisor class group.

## Key examples from Hartshorne

### Projective space
For \(\mathbf{P}^n\), the divisor class group is generated by the hyperplane class, and the Picard group is essentially \(\mathbb{Z}\).

### Curves
On a smooth projective curve:

- divisors classify line bundles via degree and linear equivalence,
- the Picard group has a degree map,
- degree-zero line bundles form the subgroup \(\mathrm{Pic}^0\), related to Jacobians.

### Surfaces and cubic surfaces
On nonsingular projective surfaces, divisor classes interact with intersection numbers, ampleness, and contractions. Hartshorne’s cubic surface example shows how the configuration of 27 lines is encoded by divisor classes and the Picard group.

## Connection to sections and morphisms

A line bundle becomes geometrically useful when it has enough global sections.

- A base-point-free linear system defines a morphism to projective space.
- A very ample line bundle defines a closed immersion.
- Effective divisors are the zero loci of global sections.

So line bundles are not just abstract objects: they are the mechanism by which varieties are embedded into projective space.

## In Hartshorne’s book

These notions are developed across the parts of the book dealing with divisors, projective geometry, and cohomology. They are used to:

- define linear systems,
- prove Riemann–Roch-type results,
- describe ampleness,
- construct morphisms to \(\mathbf{P}^n\),
- and relate geometry to cohomological invariants.
